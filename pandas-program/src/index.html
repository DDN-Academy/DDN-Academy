Read this page twice

None of this is your fault

No energy. No weight coming off. Losing muscle. Those aren’t three problems — they’re one, and it’s the opposite of what you’ve been blaming yourself for.

You are not eating too much. You’re eating far too little, and almost no protein.

What your body did

Months of too little food and a body doesn’t burn fat faster — it protects itself. It turns the thermostat down: less heat, less movement, slower digestion, foggy head, cold hands, flat mood.

And it still needs protein every day for skin, hair, hormones and immune cells. If you don’t eat any, it takes it from the only store you have — your muscle.

Why the scale stopped

A body burning less needs less. Your maintenance dropped until it met the tiny amount you were eating. That’s the stall — and it’s why eating even less has stopped working and never will again.

Muscle is also what burns calories at rest. Every kilo you lost made it harder. The way out isn’t less food. It’s more, and better built.

Three things to know before you start

1
You’ll eat more than you have in a year.
1,800 in week 1, 1,700 in week 2, then 1,600 from week 3 to the end. That is not a lot — it’s normal. It only feels enormous because you’ve been living on half of it.
2
The scale may go up in the first two weeks.
Half a kilo to a kilo. It’s water, glycogen and food in your gut refilling. At these calories it is physically impossible for it to be fat. Don’t panic, don’t cut.
3
Nothing is forbidden.
Bread, rice, pasta, potatoes, olive oil, merguez, dates, dark chocolate — all in the plan. The only things missing from how you eat were quantity and protein.

The whole plan, in one sentence

Eat three real meals, put protein in every one, walk, and train your legs hard three times a week — and your body will finally let go of fat instead of muscle, because for the first time it can afford to.

Still hungry after dinner?

Three classes · whenever the studio runs them

Your week

You don’t have to pick the days. Book whatever three slots your studio has and build the week around them. There is only one preference, and it isn’t a rule: if the timetable lets you, avoid two classes back to back. If it doesn’t, go anyway — three classes badly spaced beats two well spaced.

Class days · × 3

10,000 steps

You’re already out of the house, so these are your long-walk days too. Breakfast is compulsory and the 10:00 snack becomes the protein drink instead of the fruit.

Make at least two of the walks hilly. Madrid is built on slopes, and walking uphill is genuine glute work that costs you nothing extra. Stairs instead of the escalator, every single time — it’s the cheapest leg training in the city.

Other days · × 4

6–8,000 steps

No training at all. Eat exactly the same as a class day — rest days are when the muscle is actually built, and eating less on them is how people undo their own work.

The one thing that still happens every day, without exception, is the walking. Steps aren’t exercise; they’re the background of the day.

Never go to class fasted

Training on empty is exactly how you lose muscle instead of building it — there’s no fuel and no amino acids available, so the body takes both from your legs. Protein within two hours after.

Don’t add anything else

No running, no HIIT, no extra cardio. It eats the recovery your legs need to grow, raises your appetite sharply, and stresses a body that’s been under-fuelled for a long time. The fat comes off through food. The shape comes from class and walking.

Class cancelled?

Nothing about the food changes. Add 2,000 steps and carry on. One missed class in sixteen weeks is noise.

The part that decides the shape

Building glutes & legs

Weeks 1–4 · just go

nothing to ask, nothing to change

Take the class exactly as it’s run. Don’t negotiate with the instructor, don’t ask for a different format, don’t worry about whether it’s the “right” kind of Pilates. Your only job for the first month is turning up three times a week and eating. Everything below is available to you from day one, needs nobody’s permission — and none of it matters if you’re not in the room.

What you control inside any class, whoever is teaching it

You can’t choose the exercises — you can choose how hard each one is, and that’s what decides whether a class builds muscle or just makes you sweat.

  • Take the heavier spring. When the instructor gives a range — “blue or red” — you take the heavier one, every time, unless your form breaks.
  • Three seconds coming back. Slow the lowering phase of every repetition. It is free extra stimulus and nobody has to know you’re doing it.
  • Take the harder version. Whenever a variation is offered — single leg, arms overhead, no hands — take it.
  • Finish every leg set. Long sets are where people quietly coast. The last three reps should be genuinely hard — shaking is the point, not a problem.
  • Full range, every rep. Half a repetition builds half as much.
  • Never sit out the glute series. Bridging and side-lying work look easy and change your shape most.

The exercises worth spending yourself on when they come up

You won’t choose them, but you’ll recognise them — and this is where the heavy spring and the last hard reps go.

  • Footwork on the reformer — heels, toes, wide. Your main quad and glute lift.
  • Anything single-leg — twice the load per leg, and it exposes the weaker side.
  • Pelvic curl and bridging — the most direct glute work in the whole repertoire.
  • Feet in the straps — hip extension, circles, frogs. Slow, and fight the return.
  • Side-lying leg series — glute medius, the muscle that gives the shape from behind.
  • Lunges, squats and step-ups on the reformer or chair.

From week 5, if you feel like it — and only if you do

One sentence to the instructor at the end of a class, nothing more: “I’m trying to build strength in my legs and glutes — is there a heavier option for me?” Most will happily adjust. If the studio runs a fixed format and the answer is no, that is completely fine — the spring you pick and the tempo you use are already doing the work.

One honest thing

Three classes a week, taken the way this page describes and backed by 130 g of protein a day, will build real glute and leg muscle — especially in your first year of training and while eating properly for the first time in ages. It isn’t the fastest route that exists, but it’s the one you’ll actually do — so the three levers you do have, the heavy spring, the protein and the hills, are doing all of the work. None of them is optional.

Two supplements · four habits · one honest page

The rest of it

Whey protein

1–2 scoops a day

Going from 40 g of protein a day to 130 g is a big jump, and food alone won’t get you there while your appetite is still small. One scoop is 24 g in thirty seconds — it’s powdered milk protein, that’s all.

Half a scoop in breakfast, every day, plus one in the 10:00 shaker on Pilates days. Plain concentrate or isolate, halal mark, no gelatin — HSN, Prozis and MyProtein all deliver across Spain in a day or two. About €0.80 a day.

Vitamin D3

1,000–2,000 IU

You leave at 07:20 and you’re indoors 08:00–12:00 — most of the useful daylight, especially October to March. Low vitamin D is common in students and shows up as exactly what you’ve described.

Take it with breakfast; any farmacia sells it and a year costs under €15. That’s the complete list — fat burners, detox teas, slimming infusions, L-carnitine and CLA don’t work, and some sold online aren’t safe.

Water · 2 L

500 ml the moment you wake, before coffee. Part of the 11 a.m. tiredness is simply that you haven’t drunk anything since yesterday.

Coffee

Two a day, both before 14:00, never before food. On an empty stomach it causes the queasiness that makes breakfast impossible.

Sleep · 7.5 h

A 6:00 alarm means lights out at 22:30. Muscle is built and appetite hormones reset while you sleep. Phone across the room.

The 3 p.m. crash

A fuel problem, not your personality. Gone within ten days of eating breakfast, a 10:00 snack and a real lunch — your first proof this works.

Iron — the most likely reason you’re tired

Low ferritin is the most common cause of exhaustion in a twenty-year-old woman, and months of eating almost no meat make it likely. Easy to fix — but only once it’s been measured. Never supplement iron on a guess; too much is harmful.

What food does meanwhile: red meat, lamb, lentils, always with lemon — vitamin C roughly doubles how much iron you absorb. Still tired after four weeks of eating properly? Get the blood panel below.

Your cycle

  • The week before: appetite up, mood down. Keep the protein, eat the extra carbs without guilt, don’t weigh yourself. It passes in four days.
  • During: you lose iron — red meat or lentils, always with lemon. Keep going to class unless you’re in real pain.
  • Irregular or absent periods mean long-term under-fuelling — eat more, and see a doctor.

See a doctor if — not optional

  • The fatigue hasn’t clearly improved after four weeks of eating three real meals a day.
  • Dizziness standing up, hair falling out, cold all the time, nails breaking, bruising easily.
  • Periods that stop for three months or more.
  • Heart racing at rest, or breathless on stairs you used to manage.
  • Food starts to feel like anxiety rather than fuel — talk to someone early, it’s common and treatable.
  • Ask for this panel: ferritin, full blood count, vitamin D, B12, folate, TSH.

Guidance for a healthy twenty-year-old with no known conditions — not medical advice. If a doctor says otherwise, the doctor wins.

One last page

Read this on the bad days

“I skipped a meal.”

Eat the next one normally. That’s the whole answer. Never eat less later to balance it — that’s the exact loop that created this.

“I ate way too much.”

Enjoy it, then back to normal at the next meal. One big meal cannot undo a week. Three days of under-eating afterwards absolutely can.

“The scale went up.”

Salt, carbs, your period, training yesterday, or your muscles refilling with water. None of it is fat. Weigh again in three days.

“I’m stuck at the same weight.”

Check three things honestly: three meals, 130 g protein, the steps. Nine plateaus out of ten are one of those — and the fix is doing them, not eating less.

“I feel guilty eating this much.”
